Analytic Hessians: GFN2-xTB and g-xTB against the programs

xtb and the g-xTB programs compute Hessians by finite differences: two SCF calculations and gradients per coordinate, each converged only as far as the accuracy setting asks. axtb differentiates the energy twice analytically for GFN2-xTB (including ALPB/GBSA solvation) and g-xTB 2.0.1; the SCF's response to every displacement comes from one coupled-perturbed solve. g-xTB v1.1 stays numerical in axtb. AFH runs the programs' Hessians at --acc 0.001, the setting that makes them accurate, so that is the main comparison; their defaults are shown too.

Math libraries: what axtb uses and why

axtb writes its own chemistry kernels (integrals, Fock build, electrostatics, dispersion) and takes dense linear algebra from a library. For larger molecules most of the run time is one operation: the generalized symmetric eigenproblem of each SCF iteration.

  • Default: faer, a pure-Rust BLAS/LAPACK equivalent. Its matrix multiply and triangular solves match OpenBLAS and MKL; nothing has to be installed, packaged or configured, and its thread count is set per call on axtb's own pool, which is what keeps nested threading out. The node's timings use it.
  • On the Intel laptop the timings use Intel MKL, loaded at run time (AXTB_LAPACK) for the SCF eigensolver from — basis functions on. With faer alone, GFN2-xTB is not faster than xtb 6.8.0 in — of — laptop cells (— on — threads; the lowest —×, — on —), mostly the water clusters on several threads: there faer's diagonalization (tridiagonalization and divide and conquer), which MKL runs faster on this CPU, takes the time. g-xTB 2.0.1 stays ahead with faer (at least —×).
  • On the AMD node faer is ahead everywhere (GFN2-xTB at least —×, g-xTB 2.0.1 —×). Measured alongside: AMD AOCL at least —× and —×, Intel MKL —× and —× (energy + gradient table in "Speed").
  • The best library depends on the CPU. Intel MKL is fastest on the Intel laptop and slowest in throughput mode on the AMD Zen 5 node, where AMD AOCL and OpenBLAS lead (charts below). AOCL was built from its open sources, since AMD's prebuilt package restricts use.
  • Run-time libraries, never linked. AXTB_LAPACK names MKL, an OpenMP or sequential OpenBLAS, or AOCL's libFLAME (with AXTB_LAPACK_BLAS); axtb sets the library's thread count for each call through the library's own API, never through environment variables, and falls back to faer when the library cannot be used. MKL is never linked into the LGPL build. Smaller matrices stay on faer, and small systems keep the linear algebra sequential even when threads are allowed, because faer's parallel kernels are slower than sequential ones at that size.

GPU backend

An optional GPU eigensolver (cuSOLVER through runtime-loaded CUDA, behind a build feature) replaces the CPU eigensolver in each SCF iteration; everything else stays on the CPU threads. It is kept on a separate branch of the code, not in the main version: AFH runs many small calculations, where the CPU is faster, so it is parked until a workload needs it. Measured on the node's RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Max-Q and the laptop's RTX 3050 Ti. Both cards run FP64 at a small fraction of their FP32 rate, which is why mixed precision was tried.

  • FP64 uses the same algorithm as the CPU and reproduces it to round-off.
  • Mixed solves in FP32, then refines the occupied orbitals with FP64 steps; with enough steps it matches the CPU to well below SCF convergence. It gains less than hoped because the FP64 matrix multiplies of the refinement are themselves slow on these cards.
  • Adaptive runs FP32 while the SCF is far from converged and refines only at the end: the fastest, but it reaches the same answer by a different SCF path, so it agrees with the CPU only to SCF tolerance.
  • Small molecules stay on the CPU. Many small calculations at once serialize on one GPU; many CPU cores are far faster.

g-xTB v1.1 and 2.0.1

g-xTB is the Grimme group's newer general-purpose tight-binding method. Its source is not public, so axtb reconstructs it from the paper and its supporting information, the published parameters and the GPL-licensed programs, and checks every term against those programs. Both versions are available: method="gxtb-v1.1" and method="gxtb-v2.0.1" in Python, the command line and qccompute. Version 2.0.1 is a full refit of v1.1 with new terms (an anisotropic Hamiltonian correction, a bond-order exchange correction, charge-dependent onsite exchange, self-consistent repulsion) and covers all elements H–Lr.

  • Energies, analytic gradients and Hessians are done. Energies, charges and orbital energies follow the program's SCF iteration by iteration, including its undocumented details: a three-body dispersion term that is in the total energy but not printed, a doubled Fock matrix in its third iteration, and two Fock-matrix terms that are not the exact derivative of its energy.
  • Two conventions, as for GFN2-xTB. conventions="gxtb" reproduces the v1.1 program exactly; its gradient includes a response (Z-vector) term because the program's SCF is not stationary for its own energy. The corrected default uses the exact derivative of the energy as the Fock matrix, so the converged SCF is stationary and the gradient needs no response term. The correction lowers total energies slightly and often saves an SCF iteration (table), and it is the default: it moved every energy difference we checked by far less than the method's own accuracy, in exchange for exact and cheaper gradients.
  • Gradients are analytic in both conventions, through every term including the charge- and coordination-number-dependent basis set, and agree with finite differences of tightly converged energies. The program's own gradient is numerical and carries noise from its step size and its non-stationary SCF.

GFN-FF force field

GFN-FF is the Grimme group's general force field, set up automatically from a structure; mepd uses it for the environment in QM/MM and for large boxes. axtb's GFN-FF is a port of xtb's (LGPL) and follows xtb 6.8.0 (6.7.1's, which the g-xTB 2.0.1 build runs, as conventions xtb-6.7.1), with the topology (bonds, hybridization, π bond orders, fragments, topology charges) found the same way, then kept fixed or taken from another structure, so a whole reaction path can share one topology. It has analytic gradients and an analytic Hessian with dipole derivatives, and runs from Rust, the command line, Python and qccompute as method="gfnff".

The one structure not reproduced, D5h ferrocenium, has an exactly degenerate Hückel pair at the Fermi level, so its π bond orders depend on which eigenvectors the eigensolver returns, in xtb as well. The two xtb versions differ slightly in GFN-FF (a newer bohr constant, the π-bond threshold, the Hückel Fermi level, and the compiler's rounding of vector norms, which decides ties between equidistant neighbours); each has its own conventions in axtb. SCF convergence

axtb's SCF originally used the same Broyden mixing as tblite, so it takes the same path as the reference programs and lands on the same solution where there are several. To find out whether a better converger helps, we built a set of hard cases (bond breaking, reaction-path images, metal clusters, small gaps, open shells) and compared Broyden, a Pulay/DIIS mixer, xtb and tblite on it.

  • DIIS rescues most room-temperature failures, the kind that make reaction-path workflows retry at a raised electronic temperature, which also shifts the energy.
  • It is not a free win. In systems with several SCF solutions it sometimes converges to a different one than Broyden, once with an implausible −3 charge on an atom, and it fails one structure Broyden converges. The 0 K failures have no stable whole-electron solution, and every program fails them.
  • So the default keeps Broyden and uses DIIS only as a fallback when Broyden fails. Everything that converged before gives exactly the same result, rescued results say which mixer produced them, and the xtb and tblite modes keep pure Broyden so they still reproduce those programs, failures included.

Bugs and differences in xtb, tblite and g-xTB

Reproducing xtb and tblite to the last digits meant reading every term, which surfaced bugs in both and showed where the two programs, which implement the same GFN2-xTB method, give different numbers. Where xtb's own numbers depend on a quirk, axtb reproduces it in xtb mode so results stay identical; its default fixes it.
